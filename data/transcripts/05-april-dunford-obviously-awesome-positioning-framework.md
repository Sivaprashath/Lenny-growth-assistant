# Episode 5: April Dunford — The Obviously Awesome Positioning Framework and Sales Pitch
Guest: April Dunford (Author of 'Obviously Awesome' and 'Sales Pitch', Positioning Expert)
Host: Lenny Rachitsky

## Transcript Excerpts

Lenny Rachitsky: April, welcome to the show! Almost every founder and product marketer struggles with positioning. How do you define positioning in plain English?

April Dunford: Positioning is context setting. Humans cannot evaluate something in a vacuum. The moment you introduce a product, your customer's brain immediately looks for a mental shelf to put it on: "Is this a database? Is this email marketing? Is this an enterprise CRM?"

If you get the shelf wrong, everything that follows is wrong. If you tell an enterprise buyer you are "cool chat software for teams", they compare you to Slack and say: "Why would I pay $50k a year for chat?" But if you position the exact same technology as an "Automated Customer Support Incident Command Center", suddenly they compare you to ServiceNow or PagerDuty, where a $100k annual contract feels like an absolute bargain.

Lenny Rachitsky: What are the 5 essential components of your positioning framework?

April Dunford: Positioning consists of five interrelated pieces that must fit together like a puzzle:

1. **Competitive Alternatives**:
What would customers do if your product did not exist? Not just direct competitors, but status quo: Excel spreadsheets, hiring an intern, pen and paper, or doing nothing.

2. **Differentiated Capabilities (Unique Attributes)**:
What can your product do that the competitive alternatives cannot do? Must be verifiable facts, not marketing fluff like "we are easy to use" or "we are intuitive."

3. **Value for the Customer**:
Why do those unique capabilities matter in business terms? Capabilities are what you built; value is the dollars saved, revenue gained, or hours shaved off the workflow.

4. **Best-Fit Target Customer (ICP)**:
Who cares intensely about that specific value right now? Not everyone. Which segment has the exact pain point that makes your unique capability a no-brainer purchase?

5. **Market Category**:
What context makes your unique value obvious? The category sets the expectations for pricing, features, and competitors.

Lenny Rachitsky: What is the most dangerous trap companies fall into when crafting their pitch?

April Dunford: Starting with the product demo instead of starting with the market insight. Customers don't buy your product features; they buy your differentiated perspective on why the market changed and why their existing alternatives are failing them. Frame the change first, highlight why legacy tools fall short, and then present your solution as the only logical answer.
